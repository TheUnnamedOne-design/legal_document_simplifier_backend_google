Traditional algorithms such as K-Means, DBSCAN, and HDBSCAN either fail to capture small, irregular clusters or incur high computational costs, while deep learning-based methods like IDEC demand substantial resources. We present Nearest Neighbor Clustering (NNC), a fast and scalable algorithm that leverages   nearest   neighbor   queries,   mutual   pairing,   and   set- based processing to achieve   O ( n   log   n )   runtime while preventing singleton clusters. We   evaluate   NNC   on   synthetic   datasets   (up   to   20,000 points) and the real-world RT IOT2022 dataset (up to 50,000 points), comparing against K-Means, MiniBatchKMeans, DB- SCAN, HDBSCAN, OPTICS, BIRCH, RP-KMeans, LSH, and IDEC. Results show that NNC consistently produces fine-grained clusters with competitive cohesion and separation while signifi- cantly outperforming existing methods in runtime. By combining efficiency with cluster quality, NNC enables practical real-time analysis of IoT data, supporting tasks such as anomaly detection, traffic monitoring, and predictive resource management. I NTRODUCTION  Clustering is a fundamental technique in data analysis, widely   used   to   uncover   inherent   structures   and   patterns in   unlabeled   datasets.   In   modern   Internet-of-Things   (IoT) systems—ranging from traffic monitoring and environmental sensing to industrial automation—the ability to identify   fine- grained   spatial   patterns   is critical. Such patterns enable early anomaly detection, localized predictive maintenance, and targeted resource management. However, clustering large-scale IoT data in real time remains challenging due to the trade-off between computational efficiency and the granularity of cluster detection. Traditional algorithms face notable limitations. K-Means is   efficient   but   assumes   spherical   clusters   and   requires   a predefined number of clusters, often missing localized irreg- ular patterns. Density-based methods such as DBSCAN and HDBSCAN can detect clusters of arbitrary shapes, but they are highly sensitive to parameter choices and scale poorly on large datasets. OPTICS alleviates some sensitivity to density variation but incurs high computational cost. Deep learning ap- proaches such as IDEC can capture complex non-linear struc- tures but require substantial computational resources, making them impractical for resource-constrained IoT deployments. In contrast, fine-grained clustering is particularly valuable in IoT applications. Detecting small, dense groups of sen- sors can reveal micro-level traffic bottlenecks, localized air quality issues, or early signs of equipment failure. Ignoring such fine patterns risks masking anomalies within broader clusters, reducing the effectiveness of real-time monitoring and intervention. To address these challenges, we propose   Nearest Neigh- bor Clustering (NNC) , a lightweight and scalable algorithm that emphasizes local connectivity rather than global density assumptions. By leveraging nearest neighbor relationships, mutual pairing, and set-based leftover handling, NNC pro- duces clusters that reflect subtle spatial heterogeneities while achieving   O ( n   log   n )   runtime. The algorithm requires no user- defined global parameters, avoids trivial singletons, and adapts naturally to irregular cluster shapes. Our contributions are threefold: 1)   Algorithm   Design:   We   introduce   NNC,   a   nearest neighbor-based clustering method optimized for fine- grained and large-scale datasets. 2)   Comprehensive   Evaluation:   We   benchmark   NNC against K-Means, MiniBatchKMeans, DBSCAN, HDB- SCAN, OPTICS, BIRCH, RP-KMeans, LSH, and IDEC on synthetic and real-world IoT datasets. 3)   Insights and Analysis:   We study the effects of nor- malization vs. standardization, examine the role of fine- grained clustering in practical scenarios, and analyze the balance between cluster quality and efficiency. The results demonstrate that NNC achieves competitive clustering quality while substantially reducing runtime, mak- ing it a practical solution for large-scale, real-time IoT analyt- ics. II. N EAREST   N EIGHBOR   C LUSTERING   (NNC) A LGORITHM  Clustering large-scale spatial datasets requires balancing  efficiency, granularity, and robustness . Classical methods such as K-Means assume spherical clusters, while density- based methods like DBSCAN and HDBSCAN rely heav- ily on user-specified parameters. These limitations become pronounced in IoT or geospatial data, where cluster shapes
are irregular, densities vary, and datasets can contain tens of thousands of points. To address these challenges, we propose the   Nearest Neighbor Clustering (NNC)   algorithm, which leverages intrinsic local structures to generate fine-grained clusters with minimal computational overhead.  A. Algorithmic Insights  At its core, NNC operates on the principle that   spatial proximity   encapsulates   cluster   membership .   By   exploiting nearest neighbor relationships and mutual nearest neighbor (MNN) connections, NNC identifies natural groupings with- out requiring global density parameters or predefined cluster counts. This ensures that clusters reflect local heterogeneities, capturing fine-grained structures that traditional methods often overlook.  B. Step-by-Step Procedure  The algorithm proceeds in four main stages: 1)   Nearest Neighbor Construction:   Using a   kd-tree   or other efficient spatial index, each point identifies its nearest neighbor. This ensures   O ( n   log   n )   query time and enables rapid nearest neighbor lookups even in large-scale datasets. 2)   Seed-Based   Cluster   Formation:   Each point is pro- cessed as a potential   seed . A seed forms a cluster by aggregating all points that designate it as their nearest neighbor. This step naturally adapts to local density variations, producing compact, fine-grained clusters. 3)   Leftover Resolution and Mutual Pairing:   Points not assigned in the initial pass are processed iteratively:  •   Mutual Nearest Neighbor (MNN) Pairing:   Points that mutually recognize each other as nearest neigh- bors are grouped into minimal but stable clusters.  •   Proximity-Based Merging:   Remaining points are merged   into   nearby   clusters   based   on   centroid distances, reducing singletons and ensuring global cluster coherence. 4)   Dynamic Updates and Optimizations:  •   Reverse   Nearest   Neighbor   Indexing:   Allows constant-time retrieval of points that designate a given seed.  •   Set-Based   Processing:   Reduces   computational overhead for handling leftover points.  •   Incremental Centroid Updates:   Cluster centroids are updated as points are added, maintaining accu- rate cluster boundaries in real time.  C. Key Features and Advantages  NNC introduces several advantages over conventional meth- ods:  •   Fine-Grained Clustering:   Minimum cluster size is two, avoiding trivial singletons while capturing subtle local patterns.  •   Scalability:   Efficient nearest neighbor queries and set- based   processing   ensure   O ( n   log   n )   runtime,   making NNC suitable for tens of thousands of points.  •   Parameter   Independence:   Unlike   DBSCAN   or   OP- TICS,   NNC   requires   no   user-specified   ε ,   minPts,   or cluster count.  •   Adaptability to Irregular Shapes:   By emphasizing local proximity rather than global assumptions, NNC identifies clusters of arbitrary shape and density.  •   Robustness   to   Noise:   Sparse   or   isolated   points   are systematically merged or excluded, reducing outlier sen- sitivity. Illustrative Scenario  Consider a city-wide IoT deployment monitoring environ- mental sensors. NNC first identifies nearest neighbors for all sensors, forming clusters in densely monitored areas. Sparse sensors are merged using MNN logic or centroid-based assign- ment. The resulting clustering reflects both dense sensor hubs and irregular spatial patterns, enabling meaningful groupings for anomaly detection, traffic monitoring, or predictive mod- eling.  F. Discussion  NNC differs from conventional clustering paradigms by emphasizing   local connectivity   over global density assump- tions. It unifies the efficiency of K-Means with the flexibility of density-based methods, producing a scalable, fine-grained, and interpretable clustering framework. The algorithm’s design ensures that the structure of the data itself dictates cluster formation, resulting in clusters that are both computationally efficient and semantically meaningful. III. E XPERIMENTAL   S ETUP  To evaluate the performance of the proposed Nearest Neigh- bor Clustering (NNC) algorithm, we designed experiments that highlight   efficiency, clustering quality, and scalability . The setup uses both synthetic data and a real-world IoT dataset to ensure a balanced evaluation under controlled and practical conditions.  A. Datasets 1) Synthetic   Datasets:   We   generated   two- dimensional   uniformly   distributed   datasets   with   sizes  n   = 100 ,   1000 ,   5000 ,   10000 ,   20000 . These datasets provide controlled environments for systematically analyzing runtime scaling and clustering accuracy. Since ground truth cluster structures   are   available,   they   also   enable   evaluation   with external metrics such as ARI and NMI.  2) Real-World   IoT   Dataset   (RT IOT2022):   The RT IOT2022   dataset   consists   of   labeled   network   traffic flows collected from a large-scale IoT deployment designed for anomaly detection research [ ? ]. Each record corresponds to a bidirectional network flow and includes both statistical
traffic   features   and   protocol-level   indicators.   In   total,   the dataset contains millions of flow samples, of which subsets of   n   = 1000 ,   2000 ,   3000 ,   4000 ,   5000 , and   10000   points were selected for evaluation in this study. Key feature categories include:  •   Basic   flow   attributes:   source   and   destination   ports, protocol type, service, and flow duration.  •   Packet statistics:   total forward and backward packets, packet rates (per second), and packet size distributions.  •   Header features:   total, minimum, and maximum header sizes for forward and backward directions.  •   Payload   features:   minimum, maximum, average, and standard deviation of packet payload sizes.  •   Inter-arrival times (IAT):   forward, backward, and ag- gregated statistics (min, max, avg, std).  •   TCP flag counts:   FIN, SYN, RST, PSH, ACK, URG, CWR, and ECE occurrences.  •   Flow activity:   active and idle period statistics.  •   Window sizes:   initial and last forward/backward window sizes.  •   Label:   each flow is annotated with an   Attack_type  or benign label for ground truth reference. This rich feature set captures both temporal and statistical aspects of network traffic, making RT IOT2022 well-suited for evaluating clustering algorithms under realistic IoT traffic conditions. In our experiments, we used only the numerical features for clustering, omitting categorical fields such as protocol identifiers and attack type labels, which were reserved for external validation.  B. Baseline Parameter Settings  To ensure a fair and reproducible comparison, all baseline algorithms were configured with consistent and widely used parameter choices. Unless otherwise noted, default parame- ters from   scikit-learn   or standard implementations were used. Specific configurations are as follows:  •   K-Means / K-Means NNC / MiniBatchKMeans:   Num- ber of Unique labels in the dataset. Number of clusters  k   set to match NNC’s output. MiniBatchKMeans used a batch size of 256.  •   DBSCAN:   ε   = 0 . 1 , min samples = 5.  •   HDBSCAN:   min cluster size = 5.  •   OPTICS:   min samples = 5.  •   BIRCH:   n clusters   set to match the number of unique labels in the dataset.  •   RP-KMeans:   n clusters   as   above,   with   50   random projection components.  •   LSH:   n clusters   as above, with 10 projection compo- nents for hashing.  •   IDEC:   Encoding dimension = 10, training for 500 epochs with batch size 256. These settings were selected to balance fairness, stability, and computational feasibility. In cases where cluster counts were required (e.g., K-Means, BIRCH, RP-KMeans, LSH, IDEC), the value of   k   was aligned with NNC’s output for direct comparability. This diverse baseline set ensures that NNC is compared not only against traditional centroid- and density-based methods, but also against modern scalable and deep learning-based clustering approaches.  C. Evaluation Metrics  We   employed   both   internal   and   external   metrics   for   a holistic evaluation:  •   Compactness:   Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) and Mean Squared Error (MSE).  •   Separation:   Silhouette Score [7] and Davies–Bouldin Index (DBI) [8].  •   External Validation:   Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) [9] and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) [10], available for synthetic and semi-labeled RT IOT subsets.  •   Runtime:   Measures computational efficiency with in- creasing dataset sizes.  D. Implementation Details  All experiments were conducted on a machine with an Intel Core i9 CPU and 32 GB RAM. NNC was implemented in Python, using   scikit-learn ’s KDTree for efficient nearest neighbor queries. Baseline algorithms were executed with standard libraries and recommended parameters, except where  k   was explicitly matched with NNC. Each experiment was repeated three times, and average results were reported to mitigate system-level variability.  E. Experimental Goals  The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the perfor- mance and characteristics of the proposed Nearest Neighbor Clustering (NNC) algorithm relative to established clustering methods. To ensure that the evaluation is both systematic and reproducible, we define four experimental goals:  •   G1: Comparative performance analysis.   Benchmark NNC against a diverse set of centroid-based, density- based,   and   hybrid   clustering   algorithms,   including KMeans, MiniBatchKMeans, BIRCH, RP-KMeans, DB- SCAN, OPTICS, and LSH-based methods. The compar- ison is based on widely used cluster validity indices such as the Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin index, and Calinski–Harabasz score.  •   G2: Scalability assessment.   Investigate how the algo- rithms perform as dataset size increases. This is evaluated using the RTIOT2022 dataset with subsets of 1,000; 2,000; 3,000; 4,000; 5,000; and 10,000 points, alongside synthetic datasets that allow for controlled experimenta- tion.  •   G3: Impact of preprocessing.   Examine the effect of nor- malization and standardization on clustering outcomes. Normalization scales data to a bounded range, whereas standardization equalizes variance across features. Since clustering algorithms are sensitive to distance measures, we expect these transformations to influence performance differently.
•   G4: Fine-grained clustering evaluation.   Assess the abil- ity of NNC to retain compact and locally coherent clusters without excessive merging. This is particularly relevant for IoT scenarios where small clusters may correspond to meaningful device groups or anomalies. Validity indices that account for compactness and separation, such as the WB-index [14], are included for this purpose. By structuring the experiments around these goals, the study ensures that each aspect of clustering—quality, scal- ability, preprocessing sensitivity, and fine-grained structure preservation—is systematically addressed. Later sections of the paper revisit these goals explicitly when presenting results and discussion. V. R ESULTS AND   A NALYSIS  This section presents a focused, interpretive analysis of the experimental results. We organize the discussion around three axes: (1) behaviour on the   synthetic   datasets, (2) behaviour on the real-world   RT IOT2022   dataset under two preprocessing schemes (min–max   Normalization   and z-score   Standardiza- tion ), and (3) a comparative synthesis that identifies which algorithms prefer which preprocessing and why. Wherever useful, we cite concrete values from the experiments; full numerical results are provided in the tables.  A. Synthetic datasets  The synthetic datasets (sizes   n   = 1000   to   n   = 10000 ) contain balanced, spherical clusters, aligning with K-Means assumptions. Key findings: 1)   Accuracy   under   model   assumptions.   K-Means achieves perfect recovery of the ground truth clusters (ARI = 1.0, NMI = 1.0). By contrast, NNC attains slightly lower ARI ( ≈ 0.65–0.68) but very high NMI ( ≈ 0.96–0.97), meaning it preserves nearly all informa- tion about true groupings. 2)   Micro-cluster   formation.   NNC   discovers   thousands of   clusters   ( k   =   3848   at   n   =   10000 ),   reflecting its neighbourhood-preserving nature. This reduces ARI compared to K-Means but yields fine-grained partitions useful for anomaly detection and IoT applications. 3)   Efficiency.   NNC runs extremely fast: under   0 . 8   s at   n   = 10000 , compared to   13 . 6   s for OPTICS and   31 . 7   s for IDEC.  B. RT IOT2022: Normalization vs. Standardization  The RT IOT2022 dataset is a noisy, heterogeneous IoT benchmark. We analyze results on the largest split ( n   = 10000 ) under both preprocessing schemes. Under normalization:  •   NNC achieves a strong silhouette (0.877) and competitive DBI (0.927) with runtime 2.03 s.  •   K-Means reports the highest silhouette (0.962) but with much fewer clusters ( k   = 12 ), losing local resolution.  •   DBSCAN   and   RP-KMeans   achieve   good   silhouettes ( ≈ 0.95) but at the cost of higher DBI ( ≥ 1.00).  •   OPTICS and HDBSCAN collapse the data into broader structures, with silhouettes   ≈ 0.81–0.86 but DBI   >   0 . 92 . Under standardization:  •   NNC still records a strong silhouette (0.889), but DBI rises to 1.121 and runtime increases to 9.44 s.  •   K-Means degrades (silhouette 0.742, DBI 0.537), while DBSCAN   improves   relatively   (silhouette   0.801,   DBI 1.649).  •   HDBSCAN maintains a good silhouette (0.866) but ex- hibits instability in DBI ( > 1.84).  •   OPTICS runtime grows dramatically (39.9 s) with only moderate quality.  C. Algorithm-preprocessing preferences  From the empirical evidence:  •   Normalization is preferred   by centroid- and neighbour- based   methods   (K-Means,   MiniBatchKMeans,   RP- KMeans, NNC), which achieve higher silhouettes and lower DBI.  •   Standardization can aid   density-based methods (DB- SCAN, OPTICS, HDBSCAN), but parameter sensitivity limits plug-and-play use. In our runs, DBI values often worsened without parameter retuning.  •   Deep clustering (IDEC)   remains accurate but computa- tionally expensive, making it less suitable for IoT-scale analysis.  D. D ISCUSSION  The experiments confirm that Nearest Neighbor Clustering (NNC) achieves a strong balance between efficiency, granular- ity, and robustness. To contextualize these findings, we revisit the experimental goals outlined earlier:  G1: Efficiency  NNC demonstrated consistent   O ( n   log   n )   runtime scaling across both synthetic and real-world datasets. Figures 5–7 show that NNC maintains lower runtimes than K-Means, Mini- BatchKMeans, OPTICS, and IDEC, particularly as dataset sizes grow. This validates NNC’s suitability for real-time or resource-constrained IoT deployments.  G2: Clustering Quality  Despite its simplicity, NNC achieved competitive Silhouette scores   and   Davies–Bouldin   Index   (DBI)   values   compared to more complex algorithms. While K-Means occasionally produced slightly tighter clusters on synthetic data, NNC performed more consistently across datasets with varying den- sities, avoiding the over-generalization observed in DBSCAN and OPTICS.  G3: Normalization vs. Standardization  The comparison between normalized and standardized pre- processing revealed algorithm-dependent sensitivity. Centroid- based methods (K-Means, MiniBatchKMeans) benefitted from standardization,   while   density-based   methods   (DBSCAN, HDBSCAN) showed greater stability under normalization. NNC remained robust under both transformations, reflecting its reliance on local proximity rather than global scaling. These results align with prior studies emphasizing the importance of preprocessing in clustering performance [ ? ], [14].  G4: Fine-Grained Clustering in Practice  Fine-grained clustering proved valuable in scenarios where small, dense groups capture critical signals. For example, in IoT networks, detecting clusters of only a few sensors can reveal localized anomalies such as traffic bottlenecks, equipment faults, or air quality issues. By preventing singleton clusters and capturing subtle spatial heterogeneities, NNC enables such early detection capabilities. This perspective is consistent with findings in applied clustering research [ ? ].  Summary  Taken together, these results demonstrate that NNC bridges the gap between efficiency and granularity. It not only scales to tens of thousands of points but also preserves fine-grained local patterns that are often overlooked by traditional or deep learning-based clustering approaches. VII. C ONCLUSION AND   F UTURE   W ORK  This paper introduced Nearest Neighbor Clustering (NNC), a   scalable   algorithm   designed   for   fine-grained   clustering of   large-scale   spatial   and   IoT   datasets.   Unlike   traditional centroid- or density-based approaches, NNC leverages near- est   neighbor   relationships   and   mutual   pairing   to   achieve  O ( n   log   n )   runtime while avoiding singleton clusters. Exper- iments on both synthetic and RT IOT2022 datasets demon- strated   that   NNC   consistently   delivers   competitive   cluster quality with substantially lower runtimes compared to K- Means, MiniBatchKMeans, DBSCAN, HDBSCAN, OPTICS, BIRCH, RP-KMeans, LSH, and IDEC. The results highlight NNC’s practicality for real-time ana- lytics, where efficiency and granularity are equally important. By capturing subtle local patterns, NNC supports critical IoT applications such as anomaly detection, traffic monitoring, and predictive resource management. Future work will focus on extending NNC in three di- rections: (1) parallelization and GPU acceleration to handle millions of points in real time, (2) integration with streaming frameworks   for   dynamic,   evolving   data,   and   (3)   adaptive neighborhood selection to enhance performance on hetero- geneous datasets.
